Week 3 · Session 3SOCHUM (GA3)

Misinformation & its impact on democracies

Debate focusCombating misinformation while protecting freedom of expression.

Your first real debate — an accessible one everyone has a view on. The live tension is free expression vs. harm. If you draw a state that restricts speech "to fight misinformation," that's a devil's-country seat: argue the government's interest, not your own.

Country positions

Seats marked ★ Big are the five anchor countries for this topic — the ones your strongest delegates rotate through. The rest are supporting seats: still real, lighter prep. Everyone sits in one committee.

United States ★ Big Free expression▾
Situation

Home to the major platforms and a strong free-speech tradition. Deeply worried about foreign disinformation and election interference, but wary of government becoming an arbiter of truth — and its own politics make content moderation itself a fight.

Evidence it cites

The First Amendment; support for the UN Global Principles for Information Integrity in principle (voluntary, rights-based); backing for media freedom and journalist safety; ICCPR Article 19.

What it wants

Platform transparency and voluntary standards, real action on coordinated foreign disinformation, and firm protection of free expression — not state censorship or a 'ministry of truth'.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: EU democracies on foreign disinformation, though it diverges from them on how far to regulate platforms. Watch: accusations that it simply shields Big Tech.

Guiding questionThe US wants to fight disinformation without letting government decide what is true — is that line holdable, and who gets to draw it?
Russian Federation ★ Big Information sovereignty▾
Situation

Frames 'information sovereignty' as a security issue and uses 'fake news', 'foreign agent' and 'undesirable organisation' laws to control the domestic narrative — while also being a major exporter of state-linked disinformation.

Evidence it cites

Domestic information-control laws; sovereignty and non-interference arguments; rejection of Western 'information dominance' and platform power.

What it wants

State authority to regulate information inside its borders; reject binding international rules that could protect dissent; keep the debate on 'sovereignty', not 'rights'.

Allies / watch-outs

Loose alignment with China on information sovereignty. Watch: the gap between its 'fight fake news' language and its own disinformation exports — opponents will name it.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — can you argue 'information sovereignty' as a principle, knowing the same law shields a government from criticism?
France ★ Big Regulate platforms▾
Situation

An EU leader that treats disinformation as a threat to democracy and public health, and platform power as something to be regulated — while defending free expression as a core value.

Evidence it cites

The EU Digital Services Act (DSA) and Code of Practice on Disinformation; strong support for the UN Global Principles for Information Integrity; a media-pluralism and journalist-protection tradition.

What it wants

Binding platform duties (transparency, risk assessment, fast removal of illegal content), support for independent media, and a rights-based framing others can adopt.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: Germany and the EU bloc. Watch: Global South states who see 'the EU model' as another form of external imposition.

Guiding questionFrance wants binding rules on platforms — how does it reassure states that fear those same rules become a tool to over-remove speech?
India ★ Big Information sovereignty▾
Situation

The world's largest democracy and a vast digital market that asserts strong control over its information space — content takedowns, platform rules and frequent internet shutdowns — framed as order, security and sovereignty.

Evidence it cites

Its IT Rules (2021) pressuring platforms; a leading record on internet shutdowns; data-sovereignty and self-reliance arguments; resistance to foreign platform dominance.

What it wants

Authority to regulate platforms and content at home; technology and capacity on its own terms; caution about binding global rules it did not shape.

Allies / watch-outs

Aligns with the Global South on sovereignty and platform-power concerns; unlike Russia, it is a democracy. Watch: the tension between that democratic claim and its shutdown record.

Guiding questionIndia controls its information space in the name of democracy and order — where is the line between protecting democracy and limiting it?
Brazil ★ Big Regulate platforms▾
Situation

A large democracy that has lived through disinformation-fuelled political crisis, and now pushes platform accountability — through fierce domestic argument about censorship versus protection.

Evidence it cites

Its 'Fake News Bill' debates; high-profile clashes between its courts and platforms; Global South leadership; support for the UN Global Principles for Information Integrity.

What it wants

Platform accountability and transparency, protection of elections and democratic debate, and a Global South voice in the rules — while guarding against a censorship framing.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the EU on platform accountability; the Global South on having a say. Watch: domestic polarisation makes 'who decides' explosive.

Guiding questionBrazil saw disinformation threaten its democracy — does that justify stronger platform rules, or risk the government policing speech?
Germany Regulate platforms▾
Situation

An EU anchor with a historically grounded intolerance for hate speech and incitement, and an early mover on making platforms act.

Evidence it cites

NetzDG, its network-enforcement law, and the EU DSA; a strong data-protection and media-freedom framework.

What it wants

Binding, enforceable platform duties on illegal content, transparency, and protection of democratic discourse — inside a rights framework.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: France and the EU. Watch: free-expression critics who argue NetzDG-style laws push platforms to over-remove.

Guiding questionGermany was first to force fast removal of illegal content — did it get the balance right, or nudge platforms toward over-censoring?
Estonia Free expression▾
Situation

A small, hyper-digital democracy next to Russia, experienced at countering state-linked disinformation while fiercely protecting the free, open internet it is proud of.

Evidence it cites

Its e-state and digital-literacy programmes; NATO and EU counter-disinformation cooperation; a strong free-internet stance.

What it wants

Resilience through media literacy and transparency, coordinated defence against foreign disinformation, and a free and open internet — not censorship.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the EU, Nordic-Baltic states, and the US on foreign disinformation. Watch: how to counter hostile disinformation without copying the censor's tools.

Guiding questionEstonia faces real hostile disinformation but prizes a free internet — how do you defend the information space without closing it?
South Africa Global South▾
Situation

A leading Global South democracy where election disinformation is a live problem, wary of both unchecked platforms and Western-imposed rules.

Evidence it cites

African Union frameworks; election-integrity concerns; a consistent voice for developing-country capacity and inclusion in rule-making.

What it wants

Capacity-building, locally-appropriate solutions, and a genuine Global South seat at the table — plus accountability that isn't just the EU model exported.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the African bloc, the Global South, and Brazil and India on having a say. Watch: getting caught between the US 'free speech' and EU 'regulation' camps.

Guiding questionSouth Africa wants solutions that fit its context — what does a genuinely Global South approach to misinformation look like?
Philippines Global South▾
Situation

A democracy that became a global case study in coordinated disinformation and troll networks, with a press under pressure — the human cost of the problem made vivid.

Evidence it cites

Well-documented troll-farm and disinformation operations around its elections; threats to journalists; its ASEAN context.

What it wants

Support against coordinated disinformation and for journalist safety, platform accountability, and capacity — without tools a government could later turn on critics.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: ASEAN, the Global South, journalist-safety advocates. Watch: the same measures could be abused at home.

Guiding questionThe Philippines shows what disinformation can do to a democracy — what would actually have helped, and who should have provided it?

Key UN instruments

  • UN Global Principles for Information Integrity (2024) — five principles from the Secretary-General's Our Common Agenda.
  • Pact for the Future & Global Digital Compact (Summit of the Future, 2024).
  • ICCPR Article 19 (freedom of expression) — the anchoring standard.

Secondary reading

  • UN News on the 2024 launch; IISD SDG Knowledge Hub explainer.

NGO viewpoints

  • ARTICLE 19, Access Now — warn broad "fake news" laws chill speech and get abused.

Regional

  • EU Digital Services Act + Code of Practice on Disinformation — the leading binding model.
Week 4 · Session 4Environment (EVC1)

Marine pollution from land-based sources

Debate focusReducing marine pollution that flows from the land — plastics, wastewater, run-off.

This is your first full position statement week. The science is accessible, so put your energy into the five-step structure.

Read these as position statements

This is your position-statement week, so read each card as a model of the move you're about to make: situation → evidence → what your country wants → who it works with. Notice how the strong ones re-frame a national problem as a shared one, and match their ask to what an environment committee can realistically do.

Country positions

Seats marked ★ Big are the five anchor countries for this topic — the ones your strongest delegates rotate through. The rest are supporting seats: still real, lighter prep. Everyone sits in one committee.

Germany ★ Big High ambition▾
Situation

An EU industrial economy that treats marine plastic pollution as a systemic problem needing action at the source, not just clean-up. Backs a strong, binding global treaty.

Evidence it cites

The EU Single-Use Plastics Directive; membership of the High Ambition Coalition; UNEA Resolution 5/14's full-life-cycle mandate; UNCLOS Part XII.

What it wants

A binding treaty covering the full life cycle — production limits, controls on chemicals of concern, harmonised rules — plus support for developing states to implement.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the EU, the High Ambition Coalition, many small-island and African states. Watch: producer states and some developing economies who see caps as a brake on growth.

Guiding questionGermany wants to cut plastic at the source — how does it bring along states that read 'production caps' as a threat to their development?
Fiji ★ Big High ambition▾
Situation

A Pacific island state on the receiving end of pollution it did little to cause; plastics wash up on its shores and threaten its fisheries and tourism.

Evidence it cites

Its AOSIS and High Ambition Coalition membership; UNEA 5/14; the disproportionate harm to small-island states; ocean-health arguments.

What it wants

A strong, binding treaty with real production and chemical controls, plus finance so small states can actually act — framed as ocean justice.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: AOSIS, the High Ambition Coalition, the EU. Watch: being outweighed by big economies unless it builds a bloc.

Guiding questionFiji suffers a problem it didn't create — how does a small island turn moral weight into treaty text?
United States ★ Big Cap skeptic▾
Situation

A major consumer and waste-generating economy that has favoured national measures, innovation and waste management over binding global production caps.

Evidence it cites

A preference for country-driven, flexible approaches; emphasis on recycling and innovation; caution about binding upstream limits on its industry.

What it wants

A treaty built on national action plans and voluntary measures, with investment in waste infrastructure and innovation — not hard production caps.

Allies / watch-outs

Overlaps with producer states on caution about caps, but on cleaner grounds. Watch: pressure from ambitious states and NGOs to commit to real upstream cuts.

Guiding questionThe US wants flexibility and innovation over caps — is a treaty of national plans enough to bend the plastics curve, or a way to avoid the hard part?
Saudi Arabia ★ Big Cap skeptic▾
Situation

A leading oil producer for which plastics — a petrochemical product — are a growth market; it resists a treaty that would cap production or single out its industry.

Evidence it cites

Its petrochemical interests; sovereignty and national-circumstances arguments; a preference for downstream waste management over upstream limits.

What it wants

A narrow treaty focused on waste management and recycling, no binding production caps, and decisions by consensus — protecting the petrochemical sector.

Allies / watch-outs

Aligns with like-minded producers (Russia, Iran) on resisting caps. Watch: being cast as the blocker while small-island and ambitious states hold the moral high ground.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — follow the money: how do you argue 'waste management, not production caps' when your economy depends on making more plastic?
Indonesia ★ Big Finance & capacity▾
Situation

A vast archipelago and one of the largest sources of ocean plastic — but also a developing economy that needs the means to fix it, not just targets to meet.

Evidence it cites

UNEA 5/14; its national plastic-reduction commitments; the reality of leakage from waste systems; UNCLOS coastal-state duties.

What it wants

A treaty it can actually implement — with finance, technology transfer and capacity-building — that shares responsibility fairly between producers and consumers.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: developing states, ASEAN, others demanding means of implementation. Watch: being blamed for leakage without getting the support to stop it.

Guiding questionIndonesia is both a major source and a developing economy — how does it turn 'we need help' into a treaty that actually delivers it?
Kenya High ambition▾
Situation

An African leader on plastics — home to one of the world's strictest plastic-bag bans and to UNEP itself — and a strong voice for a binding treaty and African priorities.

Evidence it cites

Its national plastic-bag ban; hosting UNEP in Nairobi; High Ambition leanings; African Group positions.

What it wants

A strong, binding global treaty with real support for developing countries — proof that ambition and development can go together.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the African Group, the High Ambition Coalition. Watch: making sure finance flows so African states aren't left enforcing rules they can't fund.

Guiding questionKenya banned plastic bags at home — how does it use that credibility to push a global standard others resist?
Ecuador High ambition▾
Situation

A Pacific coastal state whose waters, including around the Galápagos, suffer marine pollution; conservation is tied to its identity and economy.

Evidence it cites

Galápagos protection; UNEA 5/14; regional Latin American ocean positions; UNCLOS.

What it wants

A strong treaty that protects vulnerable marine ecosystems, with fair support for developing states.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: Latin American states, the High Ambition Coalition, small-island states in spirit. Watch: balancing conservation against its own development needs.

Guiding questionEcuador guards one of the world's great marine treasures — what does it need from a treaty that clean-up alone can't provide?
China Cap skeptic▾
Situation

The world's largest plastics producer and a huge consumer; supportive of 'a treaty' in principle but cautious about binding upstream production caps, favouring national flexibility.

Evidence it cites

Its scale as a producer; a preference for downstream and national measures; sovereignty and common-but-differentiated-responsibility arguments.

What it wants

A framework treaty built on national action and flexibility, with a technology and waste-management focus — not hard, binding production limits.

Allies / watch-outs

Overlaps with producers and developing states on caution about caps. Watch: its dual role as both the biggest producer and a major victim of ocean plastic.

Guiding questionChina makes more plastic than anyone — does 'national flexibility' protect its development, or dodge the source of the problem?
India Finance & capacity▾
Situation

A large developing economy and major plastics market that stresses equity: it wants action, but insists that rich, historically high-consuming countries carry more of the burden.

Evidence it cites

Common-but-differentiated-responsibility arguments; its own plastic-waste rules; a Global South framing; UNEA 5/14.

What it wants

A treaty with finance and technology transfer, differentiated obligations, and respect for development needs — not uniform caps.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: developing states, the Global South. Watch: the tension between its equity argument and the scale of its own plastic footprint.

Guiding questionIndia argues the rich world should pay more — is that fair burden-sharing, or a reason to delay its own action?

Key UN instruments

  • UNCLOS Part XII (Art. 207) — duty to address land-based marine pollution.
  • UNEP Global Programme of Action (GPA, 1995).
  • Global plastics treaty (INC) — launched by UNEA Res. 5/14 (2022); INC-5.2 Geneva (Aug 2025) adjourned with no consensus.

Secondary reading

  • UNEP press releases; IISD Earth Negotiations Bulletin; WEF explainer.

NGO viewpoints

  • High Ambition Coalition, WWF, Greenpeace — press for production caps & controls on chemicals.

Regional

  • EU Single-Use Plastics Directive. Oil/plastic producers (Saudi Arabia, Russia) favour voluntary, waste-management measures — the core split.
Week 5 · Session 5 · worked example ★GA1 · Security Council as contrast

Maritime insecurity: piracy & the ghost fleet

Debate focusCombating piracy and the "shadow / ghost fleet" — and the hard question: who is actually allowed to stop a suspect ship?

This is the committee-powers keystone. Piracy splits neatly across two bodies, so before you read the positions, get the power distinction straight.

Committee powers — the S5 keystone

This week you debate as the General Assembly — one room, one committee. The Security Council isn't a second debate here; it's the contrast that makes the lesson land. The two bodies have very different powers:

General Assembly (GA1): recommends, urges, calls upon. It can push flag-state reform, IMO cooperation, capacity-building and sanctions coordination.
Security Council: mandates, authorises, decides (Chapter VII). Only the SC can authorise boarding or interdiction in another state's waters.
The trap: a General Assembly resolution cannot "authorise a naval operation" or "order" ships to be boarded — that power belongs to the Security Council (see the Somalia resolutions 1816 & 1851). Notice who needs the Council and who can act through the GA, the IMO, or their own ports. In the spring, a small group runs this as a real Security Council — see the Spring tab.

Country positions

Seats marked ★ Big are the five anchor countries for this topic — the ones your strongest delegates rotate through. The rest are supporting seats: still real, lighter prep. Everyone sits in one committee.

United States ★ Big Strong enforcement▾
Situation

Reads the shadow fleet as both a sanctions-evasion tool (Russia, Iran, Venezuela) and a security and environmental hazard. Wants to protect freedom of navigation and choke off illicit oil revenue.

Evidence it cites

Led the G7 oil price cap; helped impose 900+ shadow-fleet sanctions in 2025; UNCLOS right of visit (Art. 110); the Somalia precedent (SC Res. 1816/1851) for Council-authorised action.

What it wants

Tougher flag-state accountability, port-state inspections, expanded sanctions, and — where lawful — SC-authorised interdiction of suspect vessels.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: UK, EU, Estonia. Watch: any wording that needs Security Council consensus can be blocked by a Russian or Chinese veto.

Guiding questionThe US can act hard bilaterally — but can a General Assembly resolution authorise boarding a ship? If not, what should the US ask the GA to do instead?
United Kingdom Strong enforcement▾
Situation

Concerned with Baltic and North Sea security, undersea-cable protection, and the integrity of shipping insurance and registries centred in London.

Evidence it cites

Shadow-fleet sanctions; work on a legal basis to detain suspect tankers; IMO Resolution A.1192(33) (2023) on the "dark fleet"; UNCLOS flag-state duties (Art. 94).

What it wants

Coordinated flag-state reform, port-state control, an IMO-led push on registry integrity, and targeted sanctions.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: US, EU, Estonia. Watch: sovereignty objections from open-registry flag states.

Guiding questionIf the problem is ships hiding behind weak flags, what can the IMO realistically require — and what still needs the Security Council?
Estonia Strong enforcement▾
Situation

On the front line for Baltic shadow-fleet transits and undersea cable/pipeline damage. A small state whose security depends on the rules-based order holding.

Evidence it cites

Baltic cable-damage incidents; NATO and EU coordination; UNCLOS right of visit; the risk from uninsured, ageing "dark" tankers.

What it wants

A recognised basis to inspect suspect, uninsured, "dark" vessels; stronger EU/NATO coordination; and an environmental-risk framing that isolates the polluters.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: EU, UK, US, Nordic states. Watch: high-seas freedom-of-navigation limits on how far you can go to board a ship.

Guiding questionA small state can't police the sea alone — how does Estonia turn a national worry into a resolution the whole committee will back?
Russian Federation ★ Big Sovereignty-first▾
Situation

The shadow fleet is largely Russia's own workaround for the G7 price cap. It frames Western sanctions as illegitimate and boarding of its ships as unlawful.

Evidence it cites

UNCLOS freedom of navigation and flag-state exclusivity (Art. 92); the argument that sanctions and interdictions lack Security Council authorisation.

What it wants

Reject the "shadow fleet" label; oppose any SC interdiction mandate; steer the text toward general safety and environmental cooperation that names no state.

Allies / watch-outs

Partial alignment with China and Iran. Watch: the environmental-safety argument can be turned against you by coastal states.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — can you argue Russia's freedom-of-navigation case convincingly, whatever you personally think?
China ★ Big Sovereignty-first▾
Situation

A major buyer of discounted Russian and Iranian oil, but also a country with real anti-piracy credentials from its Gulf of Aden escort missions. It guards freedom of navigation and opposes unilateral sanctions.

Evidence it cites

UNCLOS freedom of navigation; its own naval escort record; consistent opposition to "long-arm" unilateral sanctions.

What it wants

Cooperative, UN-mandated anti-piracy: yes. Unilateral interdiction and sanctions: no. Protect commercial shipping and sovereignty.

Allies / watch-outs

Partial with Russia; but shares an interest in safe sea lanes with almost everyone. Watch: the gap between its anti-piracy record and its shadow-oil purchases.

Guiding questionChina both fights piracy and buys shadow-fleet oil — how do you hold that line credibly in committee?
Singapore ★ Big Coastal & capacity▾
Situation

Guardian of the Malacca–Singapore Strait, one of the busiest chokepoints on earth. Any disruption — piracy or a great-power stand-off — hits its economy directly.

Evidence it cites

ReCAAP, the regional information-sharing centre it hosts; cooperative littoral patrols; the UNCLOS transit-passage regime.

What it wants

Practical cooperation — information-sharing, coordinated patrols, capacity-building — and real caution about militarising straits; firm sovereignty over its territorial waters.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: ASEAN littoral states (Indonesia, Malaysia). Watch: being squeezed between US and Chinese framings of the problem.

Guiding questionSingapore's home strait is the model that works — how does it sell cooperative patrols to a committee that's tempted by tougher enforcement?
Somalia ★ Big Coastal & capacity▾
Situation

Birthplace of the modern piracy debate. Wants the committee to remember that piracy grew from illegal fishing, dumping and state collapse off its coast — not just criminality.

Evidence it cites

The Somalia SC resolutions (1816, 1846, 1851) that authorised action in its waters; continuing coast-guard capacity gaps; illegal-fishing grievances.

What it wants

Coast-guard capacity-building, action on illegal fishing, root-cause investment, and respect for its sovereignty — not open-ended foreign navies.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: African bloc, Gulf of Guinea states. Watch: foreign operations in its waters without consent.

Guiding questionSomalia's waters set the precedent for SC-authorised action — does that precedent protect Somalia, or override it?
Nigeria Coastal & capacity▾
Situation

The Gulf of Guinea has been a piracy and kidnap-for-ransom hotspot, driven by onshore problems — oil theft, unemployment — that feed offshore crime.

Evidence it cites

The Yaoundé Code of Conduct; its national Deep Blue maritime-security project; enforcement within its EEZ under UNCLOS.

What it wants

Regional coordination, capacity-building, and recognition that enforcement must respect coastal-state jurisdiction — plus attention to onshore causes.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: Ghana, Gulf of Guinea states, the African Union. Watch: foreign navies operating inside its EEZ.

Guiding questionGulf of Guinea piracy fell sharply when the region coordinated — what can a global resolution add without stepping on coastal-state sovereignty?
Panama Flag-state / registry▾
Situation

Runs the world's largest ship registry. Every "flag-state reform" proposal lands squarely on it, and it doesn't want its registry — a major part of its economy — punished.

Evidence it cites

UNCLOS flag-state duties and the "genuine link" debate (Art. 91–94); its IMO membership; its own de-flagging of sanctioned vessels under pressure.

What it wants

IMO-led registry standards it can actually meet, technical assistance and due process — not punitive, unilateral flag blacklists.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: other open registries (Liberia, Marshall Islands). Watch: being scapegoated for the entire shadow fleet.

Guiding questionIf "weak flags" are the problem, is the fix punishing registries — or funding and standardising them? And whose job is that: the GA, the IMO, or the Council?

Key UN instruments

  • UNCLOS Art. 100–107 (piracy), 105 (seizure on the high seas), 110 (right of visit), 91–94 (flag-state duties).
  • Security Council Somalia resolutions 1816, 1846, 1851 — authorised action in Somali waters.
  • IMO Resolution A.1192(33) (2023) — first defined and targeted the "dark fleet."

Secondary reading

  • Atlantic Council and RUSI (flag-state reform); UN News and BBC on Baltic and Gulf of Guinea incidents.

NGO / industry viewpoints

  • BIMCO and the International Chamber of Shipping on seafarer safety and registry integrity. Think-tank estimate: ~1,140 shadow tankers by Aug 2025 (~18% of the global oil-tanker fleet).

Regional pacts

  • EU shadow-fleet sanctions & tanker seizures; G7 oil price cap; Djibouti Code (Gulf of Aden); Yaoundé Code (Gulf of Guinea); ReCAAP (Malacca & Singapore Strait).
Week 6 · Session 6Disarmament / GA1

Regulating autonomous weapon systems (LAWS)

Debate focusShould the UN move toward a binding treaty on autonomous weapons — and what does "meaningful human control" require?

A verb-audit week: the real UN texts here are full of precise, arguable language. Expect a devil's-country seat for a major weapons developer resisting a ban.

This is your verb-audit week

The UN text on autonomous weapons is unusually precise, and states fight over exact wording — “meaningful human control”, a “two-tier” approach (prohibit some systems, regulate the rest), “prohibit” versus “regulate” versus “further study”. As you read the seats, notice which verbs each side wants in the text and which it wants kept out: that gap is the whole debate.

Country positions

Seats marked ★ Big are the five anchor countries for this topic — the ones your strongest delegates rotate through. The rest are supporting seats: still real, lighter prep. Everyone sits in one committee.

United States ★ Big Regulate, not ban▾
Situation

A leading developer of military AI that supports responsible-use norms but opposes a binding treaty — or a mandate to start treaty talks — for fear of constraining its own capabilities.

Evidence it cites

Its 2023 Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI (50+ states); existing international humanitarian law; a preference for CCW consensus over a new instrument.

What it wants

Voluntary norms and best practice under existing law; no binding prohibition or negotiation mandate; keep the CCW as the forum.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: Political Declaration endorsers, and other developers wary of a ban. Watch: the Secretary-General and 160+ states calling for a binding instrument.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — can you argue 'existing law is enough' to a room that says autonomous killing needs a new treaty?
Russian Federation ★ Big Reject controls▾
Situation

A major developer that voted against the GA resolutions on autonomous weapons and rejects new constraints as premature and politicised.

Evidence it cites

Arguments that IHL already applies; opposition to the GA process; insistence on consensus-only work inside the CCW.

What it wants

Block binding measures and negotiation mandates; keep any discussion slow, technical and consensus-bound; protect its own development.

Allies / watch-outs

Voted against alongside Belarus and North Korea. Watch: near-total isolation — 160+ states voted the other way.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — how do you argue 'not now, not here' when almost the whole committee wants to move?
China ★ Big Regulate, not ban▾
Situation

A major military-AI developer that abstains: it acknowledges the risks and even calls for limits in principle, but wants technological maturity before binding commitments — while preserving its own programmes.

Evidence it cites

Its abstention record; statements favouring caution and 'further study'; sovereignty and preserving strategic options.

What it wants

More discussion and definition first; non-binding or consensus outcomes; space to keep developing; no hard prohibition now.

Allies / watch-outs

Overlaps with the US and India on caution about binding limits, though it frames it differently. Watch: the gap between calling for limits and abstaining on them.

Guiding questionChina says it wants limits but abstains on them — is 'not yet' a principled stance or a way to keep its options open?
Austria ★ Big Ban it▾
Situation

A leading champion of a binding treaty; hosted a major 2024 conference to build momentum for prohibition and regulation.

Evidence it cites

Its convening role; the Secretary-General's call for a binding instrument by 2026; the two-tier approach (prohibit some systems, regulate the rest); meaningful human control.

What it wants

Recognising the crime of ecocide

Debate focusShould "ecocide" become the fifth international crime under the Rome Statute?

Operative-clause week with a built-in trap. Remember: the GA cannot amend the Rome Statute or refer cases — only the ICC's Assembly of States Parties can. Don't write "refers to the ICC" in a GA resolution.

The referral trap — read this first

This is your operative-clause week, and it hides the term's cleanest trap. Ecocide isn't law yet — it's a proposed amendment to the Rome Statute. The catch: the General Assembly cannot amend the Rome Statute, and it cannot refer anyone to the International Criminal Court. Only the ICC's Assembly of States Parties can add the crime (by a two-thirds vote), and only the Security Council or a state party can refer a situation.

The trap: almost everyone reaches for a clause that “refers perpetrators to the ICC” or “establishes ecocide as a crime.” A GA resolution can do neither — it can only urge, encourage and call upon states to support the amendment. As you read the seats, notice who wants the GA to overreach and who knows its limits.

Allies: Greece, Spain, Malta on burden-sharing. Watch: criticism that its deals and NGO restrictions risk returning people to danger.

Guiding questionItaly is bound to rescue but overwhelmed at home — where is the line between managing arrivals and failing the duty to rescue?
Greece ★ Big Frontline state▾
Situation

A frontline state for Aegean and Mediterranean crossings, under constant pressure and facing repeated allegations of pushbacks; it too demands European solidarity.

Evidence it cites

UNCLOS, SOLAS and SAR duties; the EU Pact; documented pushback allegations; the 2023 Pylos shipwreck.

What it wants

EU solidarity and relocation, control of its borders, and cooperation with Türkiye and origin states — while contesting the pushback allegations.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: Italy, Spain, other frontline states. Watch: human-rights scrutiny over pushbacks and non-refoulement.

Guiding questionGreece insists it protects its borders lawfully — how does a delegate defend that against credible allegations of pushbacks?
Spain ★ Big Frontline state▾
Situation

A frontline state on the Atlantic (Canary Islands) and Mediterranean routes; generally more cooperative with origin countries, but still a major arrival point demanding shared responsibility.

Evidence it cites

UNCLOS, SOLAS and SAR duties; cooperation agreements with West African states; the surge on the Canary Islands route.

What it wants

Managed, cooperative migration with origin and transit states, EU solidarity, and safe pathways — working 'upstream' rather than only at the shore.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: EU frontline states, West African partners. Watch: whether upstream cooperation quietly becomes externalised border control.

Guiding questionSpain works with origin countries to manage the route — is that partnership, or outsourcing its border, and does it protect people or just move the problem?
Germany ★ Big Rescue & rights▾
Situation

A major destination state that has taken in large numbers of refugees and pushes for a shared, rules-based European response with humanitarian standards — while domestic pressure has hardened its stance.

Evidence it cites

The 1951 Refugee Convention and non-refoulement; the EU Pact and relocation mechanisms; a relatively rights-forward tradition.

What it wants

A fair EU relocation system, upheld rescue and non-refoulement obligations, safe and legal pathways, and solidarity so frontline states aren't left alone.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the EU, rights-forward and resettlement states, UNHCR. Watch: balancing humanitarian commitments against domestic political limits.

Guiding questionGermany wants both solidarity and standards — how does it get frontline states to rescue and share when destination states won't take enough people?
Türkiye ★ Big Deterrence▾
Situation

Host to one of the world's largest refugee populations, it holds real leverage over flows to Europe and has used it — most visibly through the 2016 EU-Türkiye deal.

Evidence it cites

Its huge hosting burden; the EU-Türkiye Statement; its position as a transit and host state; sovereignty arguments.

What it wants

Recognition and funding for its hosting burden, cooperation on its own terms, and leverage preserved — resisting being cast simply as Europe's gatekeeper.

Allies / watch-outs

Aligns with other host and transit states seeking recognition. Watch: criticism that using migration as leverage puts people's safety in play.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — Türkiye carries a massive burden and holds real leverage — how do you argue for recognition without treating desperate people as a bargaining chip?
Canada Rescue & rights▾
Situation

A resettlement leader far from the crossing routes, which champions orderly, rights-based responses and safe legal pathways as the humane alternative to dangerous sea journeys.

Evidence it cites

Its refugee-resettlement programmes; the 1951 Refugee Convention; the Global Compact for Migration; a humanitarian-diplomacy tradition.

What it wants

Expanded safe and legal pathways, resettlement and burden-sharing, upheld rescue and non-refoulement duties, and root-cause investment.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: UNHCR, rights-forward states, Germany. Watch: being told resettlement is easy from a country with no sea border to police.

Guiding questionCanada offers safe pathways as the answer — is that a real solution at the scale needed, or a comfortable position for a country far from the shore?
Egypt Deterrence▾
Situation

A pivotal transit country and, increasingly, a partner Europe pays to stem departures; it frames migration control as a service it provides and a matter of its own stability.

Evidence it cites

Its transit position; EU cooperation and funding arrangements; sovereignty and stability arguments.

What it wants

Funding and recognition for controlling departures, cooperation on its own terms, and support for its economy and stability — not obligations that strain it.

Allies / watch-outs

Aligns with transit states seeking payment and recognition. Watch: whether externalised control meets human-rights standards.

Guiding questionEurope pays Egypt to stop departures — who is accountable for what happens to people stopped along the way?
Nigeria Origin & transit▾
Situation

A major origin country whose nationals attempt the deadly Central Mediterranean route; it wants its people protected and the drivers of migration — hardship, insecurity — addressed.

Evidence it cites

The Global Compact for Migration; consular-protection concerns; the reality of its nationals on the route.

What it wants

Protection and dignity for its citizens, safe pathways, root-cause investment, and cooperation that isn't just Europe pushing its border south.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: African origin states, the African Union. Watch: balancing cooperation on returns against protecting its people.

Guiding questionNigeria wants its citizens protected and the causes addressed — how does it push back on being made a border guard while still cooperating?
Indonesia Origin & transit▾
Situation

A key state in the Southeast Asian dimension of the crisis, on the route of Rohingya and other boats in the Andaman Sea and Bay of Bengal; it faces the same rescue-versus-capacity dilemma far from the Mediterranean.

Evidence it cites

The Bali Process; regional disembarkation dilemmas; UNCLOS and SAR duties; the plight of Rohingya at sea.

What it wants

Regional burden-sharing, support and capacity, and shared responsibility across ASEAN and beyond — so no single state is left to receive everyone.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: ASEAN, Bali Process partners. Watch: regional reluctance to disembark, and the gap between rescue duties and capacity.

Guiding questionThe crisis isn't only the Mediterranean — what does Indonesia's experience add to a resolution written mostly with Europe in mind?

Key UN instruments

  • Duty to assist: UNCLOS Art. 98; SOLAS (1974); SAR Convention (1979).
  • 1951 Refugee Convention (non-refoulement); Global Compact for Migration (2018); "place of safety" guidance.

Spring · BISSMUN → SCANDMUNSOCHUM (GA3) / SDC

Climate impacts on coastal communities / rising seas

Debate focusWhat does the world owe low-lying and coastal states as the sea rises — and can a state survive the loss of its land?

This one waits for the spring, on the road to SCANDMUN. Existential stakes and a clean small-island-vs-large-emitter divide make it a strong debate to come back to.

The question no committee has answered

This debate carries a question international law is still working out: can a state survive the loss of its land? If a nation's territory goes under, do its statehood, its UN seat and its maritime zones survive with it? The 2025 ICJ advisory opinion and the Pacific Islands Forum declarations (both linked below) point one way; the big emitters are wary of where those answers lead. Notice which seats want the questions settled and which want them left open.

Country positions

Seats marked ★ Big are the five anchor countries for this topic — the ones your strongest delegates rotate through. The rest are supporting seats: still real, lighter prep. Everyone sits in one committee.

Tuvalu ★ Big Existential / SIDS▾
Situation

One of the most sea-level-threatened states on earth; parts may become uninhabitable within a generation, so it is pioneering the argument that its statehood must continue even if its land does not.

Evidence it cites

Its constitutional and diplomatic moves to preserve statehood ('digital nationhood'); Pacific Islands Forum declarations on maritime zones and statehood; the ICJ advisory opinion; AOSIS membership.

What it wants

A firm principle that statehood, UN membership and maritime zones continue despite sea-level rise; loss-and-damage finance; and deep, fast emissions cuts by the big emitters.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: AOSIS, Pacific small-island states, the Maldives. Watch: big emitters wary of anything that looks like accepting liability.

Guiding questionTuvalu is asking the world to agree its nation survives even underwater — what would it take to write that into a resolution?
Maldives ★ Big Existential / SIDS▾
Situation

A low-lying Indian Ocean nation where the highest ground is a couple of metres above the sea; a long-standing moral voice warning that this is about survival, not abstraction.

Evidence it cites

Its decades of climate diplomacy; AOSIS leadership; continuity-of-statehood arguments; the ICJ advisory opinion.

What it wants

Recognition of continuity of statehood and maritime baselines, adaptation and loss-and-damage finance, and binding emissions cuts.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: AOSIS, small-island states, climate-vulnerable states. Watch: the gap between moral urgency and what big emitters will actually fund.

Guiding questionThe Maldives has warned the world for decades — how does it make a committee feel a slow-motion emergency that isn't in the headlines?
United States ★ Big Large emitter▾
Situation

The largest historical emitter and a wealthy power that supports adaptation and (cautiously) the Loss and Damage Fund, but resists anything framed as legal liability or compensation for climate harm.

Evidence it cites

The Paris Agreement; contributions to climate finance; a consistent line against binding liability or compensation language.

What it wants

Voluntary action, adaptation and finance support, and no admission of legal responsibility — keeping the debate on cooperation, not compensation.

Allies / watch-outs

Overlaps with other big emitters wary of liability. Watch: small-island states and the ICJ opinion pushing toward legal obligation.

Guiding questionDevil's-country test — how do you offer sympathy and some money while refusing the word 'liability' that the vulnerable states most want?
China ★ Big Large emitter▾
Situation

The largest current emitter, which frames itself as a developing country under common-but-differentiated responsibilities and positions itself as a partner to the Global South on climate.

Evidence it cites

The Paris Agreement and CBDR arguments; its South-South climate cooperation; its developing-country self-framing.

What it wants

Recognition of differentiated responsibility, developed-country finance leadership, flexibility on its own timelines, and no binding liability — while courting small-island and developing states.

Allies / watch-outs

Courts the Global South; overlaps with big emitters on avoiding liability. Watch: the tension between being the top current emitter and claiming developing-country status.

Guiding questionChina is the biggest emitter but calls itself developing — how does a delegate hold that line to a room full of sinking islands?
Netherlands ★ Big Adaptation partner▾
Situation

A wealthy, low-lying state that has lived with the sea for centuries and become the world's model for adaptation — but is also a developed emitter with obligations of its own.

Evidence it cites

Its centuries of flood defence and delta engineering; EU climate policy; the Paris Agreement; a tradition of sharing water expertise.

What it wants

Big investment in adaptation and resilience, knowledge- and technology-sharing, ambitious mitigation, and support for vulnerable coastal states.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the EU, adaptation-focused states, the vulnerable states it assists. Watch: whether adaptation talk lets big emitters off the hook on cutting emissions.

Guiding questionThe Netherlands proves you can hold back the sea — but it's rich; does 'adapt like us' work for a state that can't afford sea walls?
Fiji Existential / SIDS▾
Situation

A Pacific leader on climate and oceans that has chaired global climate talks and speaks for the region's survival and dignity.

Evidence it cites

Its COP leadership; AOSIS and Pacific Islands Forum roles; the 2021 and 2023 PIF declarations; ocean and climate-justice framing.

What it wants

Ambitious emissions cuts, loss-and-damage finance, continuity of statehood and maritime zones, and a strong, united Pacific voice.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: AOSIS, Pacific small-island states, the EU on ambition. Watch: keeping global attention on a region far from the big capitals.

Guiding questionFiji has led global climate talks — how does it convert that credibility into commitments that outlast the conference?
Australia Adaptation partner▾
Situation

A wealthy Pacific neighbour and adaptation supporter that has stepped up its climate diplomacy — but remains a major coal and gas exporter, a tension its Pacific neighbours point out.

Evidence it cites

The Paris Agreement; its Pacific partnerships and climate-finance pledges; its fossil-fuel export economy.

What it wants

To be the Pacific's partner of choice on adaptation and finance, ambitious-but-manageable mitigation, and regional stability — while protecting its export economy.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the Pacific states it funds, developed adaptation supporters. Watch: the gap between its Pacific-partner rhetoric and its coal and gas exports.

Guiding questionAustralia wants to be the Pacific's friend and a big fossil-fuel exporter at once — can it be both in a room full of sinking neighbours?
India Coastal & finance▾
Situation

A large developing state with a vast, vulnerable coastal population and huge cities at risk; it insists on climate finance and differentiated responsibility rather than being lumped with historic emitters.

Evidence it cites

CBDR arguments; the Paris Agreement; its development and energy-access needs; its exposed coastline and deltas.

What it wants

Developed-country finance and technology, differentiated obligations, adaptation support for its coasts, and recognition of its development needs.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: the Global South, developing coastal states. Watch: balancing its own rising emissions against its developing-country framing.

Guiding questionIndia has hundreds of millions on vulnerable coasts and rising emissions — how does it demand finance without ducking its own trajectory?
Indonesia Coastal & finance▾
Situation

A vast archipelago whose capital, Jakarta, is literally sinking — an emblem of the coastal-city crisis — and which is relocating its capital in response.

Evidence it cites

The sinking of Jakarta and its capital relocation; CBDR and developing-country framing; its exposed archipelago and coastal populations.

What it wants

Adaptation and relocation finance, technology and capacity, differentiated responsibility, and recognition that even large developing states can't fund this alone.

Allies / watch-outs

Allies: developing coastal states, ASEAN, the Global South. Watch: balancing its own emissions and development against its finance demands.

Guiding questionIndonesia is moving its capital because the old one is drowning — what does that tell a committee about the scale and cost of adaptation?

  • ICJ Advisory Opinion (July 2025) on states' climate obligations.
